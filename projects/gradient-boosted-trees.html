Gradient-boosted decision trees are a strong practical choice for structured data, where useful signals often live in a mix of numeric, categorical and behavioural features. The framework here treats prediction as one part of a decision workflow: the split must reflect the real use case, the metric must match the action, and model scores must not be mistaken for causal impact.

01 / OBJECTIVEPredictive scores for decisions

In customer ranking, a model can estimate which customers are more likely to take an action—such as purchasing a vehicle or leaving a service. A ranked list can help focus limited outreach capacity on the people with the highest predicted propensity. Gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) are useful in this setting because they learn non-linear relationships and interactions from structured, tabular data without requiring every relationship to be specified in advance.

A strong benchmark score is only evidence about performance on a particular dataset and evaluation design. It does not, by itself, establish that a campaign will create additional purchases, reduce churn or improve business outcomes.

02 / WORKFLOWBuild a trustworthy tabular workflow

Start with leakage-safe validation

Define the prediction moment and the outcome window before preparing features. Every input must have been available at the time the prediction would be made. Keep target-derived fields and post-outcome information out of the feature set, and fit transformations using training data only. Use a validation design that respects the data-generating process—for example, keeping related records together or evaluating later periods when that matches deployment.

Engineer features that reflect the decision

Feature work should clarify customer history and context without smuggling in information that would not exist at scoring time. Useful candidates can include recency, frequency, tenure, changes in behaviour and carefully defined interactions. Check missingness and category handling, and keep every feature's availability and meaning auditable. More features are not automatically better; each should be defensible and reproducible.

Compare diverse models, then blend with discipline

Tree-boosting variants can behave differently across datasets, so compare candidates using the same leakage-safe split and the metric that matters. A blend or stack is worthwhile only when its gains are stable out of sample. Generate training predictions for a stack in an out-of-fold manner, reserve an untouched test set for final evaluation, and avoid repeatedly tuning against that final set. Added complexity is not a substitute for reliable validation.

03 / EVALUATIONChoose metrics around the action

No single metric answers every operational question. The evaluation should reflect what the organisation will do with the predictions and how outcomes are measured:

  • ROC-AUC measures how well scores rank positive examples above negative examples across thresholds. It is useful for broad discrimination comparisons, but does not tell you how many positives appear in the limited group you can contact.
  • Top-K or precision/recall at K focuses on the highest-ranked customers when capacity is limited. Choose K to reflect a realistic contact budget, and compare against a transparent baseline.
  • Calibration checks whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcome frequencies. This matters when a decision depends on expected value, thresholds or communicating risk—not just ordering.
  • Accuracy can be informative at a specified threshold, but it may obscure class imbalance and is sensitive to the threshold chosen.

Report the split strategy, baseline, sample context and metric definition alongside a score. If probabilities inform decisions, evaluate calibration on held-out data and recalibrate only using a process that preserves separation from the test set.

The EV purchase and telecom churn datasets are synthetic. Their metrics demonstrate benchmark performance under the stated evaluation setup; they do not show that targeting customers with a model caused more sales or prevented churn in a live market.

05 / CAUSAL LIMITSPropensity is not uplift

A propensity model estimates who is likely to act. Those customers may have acted even without contact. An uplift model instead aims to estimate how an intervention changes an individual's outcome relative to what would have happened without that intervention. The two rankings can differ: the most likely customers to purchase are not necessarily the customers whose purchase is most influenced by a campaign.

To establish incremental campaign effect, run a controlled experiment with a suitable untreated comparison group, a pre-defined outcome and analysis plan, and enough observations to measure the effect with appropriate uncertainty. Monitor operational costs and guardrails as well as response. Predictive benchmark scores can inform a test; they cannot replace one.

These distinctions make the GBDT workflow useful as a disciplined prioritisation tool while keeping the decision-maker clear about what has—and has not—been demonstrated.
